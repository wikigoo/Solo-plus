[شیوع](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outbreak "شیوع") شدید وبا در سال 1854 در نزدیکی خیابان براد (که اکنون [خیابان برادویک نامیده می‌شود](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadwick_Street "خیابان برادویک") [)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholera "وبا") و [میدان گلدن](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Square "میدان طلایی") در [سوهو](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho "سوهو") ، [لندن](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "لندن") ، انگلستان، در طول همه‌گیری جهانی [وبا 1846-1860](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1846%E2%80%931860_cholera_pandemic "همه‌گیری وبا ۱۸۴۶–۱۸۶۰") رخ داد. این شیوع (که با نام **شیوع میدان گلدن** [^1] [^1] [^1] نیز شناخته می‌شود ) که منجر به کشته شدن 420 نفر شد، بیشتر به خاطر مطالعه پزشک [جان اسنو](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Snow "جان اسنو") در مورد علل آن و فرضیه او مبنی بر اینکه [آب آلوده به میکروب](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_theory_of_disease "نظریه میکروبی بیماری") علت آن است، نه چیزی در هوا به نام " [میاسما](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miasma_theory "نظریه میاسما") " شناخته می‌شود. [^2] [^2] [^2] [^3] [^3] [^3] این کشف از اواسط قرن نوزدهم بر [سلامت عمومی](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health "بهداشت عمومی") و ساخت تأسیسات [بهداشتی](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation "بهداشت") بهبود یافته تأثیر گذاشت. بعدها، اصطلاح " [کانون عفونت](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_of_infection "کانون عفونت") " برای توصیف مکان‌هایی مانند پمپ خیابان براد، که شرایط برای انتقال عفونت مساعد است، استفاده شد. اسنو در طول تلاش‌های خود برای شناسایی علت انتقال وبا، ناآگاهانه از یک [آزمایش طبیعی](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_experiment "آزمایش طبیعی") بهره برد.

## پیشینه

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Punch-A_Court_for_King_Cholera.png)

دادگاهی برای شاه وبا. تصویر از پانچ (۱۸۵۲).

در اواسط قرن نوزدهم، سوهو در لندن به دلیل هجوم زیاد مردم و فقدان خدمات بهداشتی مناسب، با مشکل جدی آلودگی مواجه بود: سیستم فاضلاب لندن به سوهو نرسیده بود. طویله‌های گاو، کشتارگاه‌ها و لانه‌های جوشان چربی در خیابان‌ها قرار داشتند و فضولات حیوانات، مایعات فاسد و سایر آلاینده‌ها را به سیستم فاضلاب ابتدایی سوهو وارد می‌کردند. [^4] بسیاری از [زیرزمین‌ها](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basement "زیرزمین") در زیر تخته‌های کف خود دارای [چاه‌های فاضلاب](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesspool "سسپول") بودند که از فاضلاب‌ها و آلودگی‌هایی که از بیرون به داخل نفوذ می‌کردند، تشکیل می‌شدند. [^4] با سرریز شدن چاه‌های فاضلاب، دولت لندن تصمیم گرفت زباله‌ها را به [رودخانه تیمز](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames "رودخانه تیمز") بریزد و منبع آب را آلوده کند. [^5] لندن پیش از این "سری شیوع‌های وبا ناتوان‌کننده" را تجربه کرده بود. [^6] این موارد شامل شیوع‌هایی در سال‌های 1832 و 1849 بود که منجر به مرگ 14137 نفر شد. [^6]

## نظریه‌های رقیب در مورد وبا

در زمان شیوع بیماری در خیابان براد، پزشکان و دانشمندان دو نظریه رقیب در مورد علل وبا در بدن انسان داشتند: نظریه میاسما و نظریه میکروب. [^7] جامعه پزشکی لندن در مورد علت شیوع مداوم وبا در این شهر بحث می‌کرد. باکتری عامل وبا، *[ویبریو کلرا،](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrio_cholerae "ویبریو کلرا")* در سال 1854 جدا شد، اما این یافته تنها چند دهه بعد شناخته و پذیرفته شد. [^8]

### نظریه میاسما

نظریه‌پردازان میاسما به این نتیجه رسیدند که وبا ناشی از ذرات موجود در هوا یا "میاسماتا" است که از تجزیه مواد یا سایر منابع آلی کثیف ناشی می‌شوند. تصور می‌شد ذرات "میاسما" از طریق هوا حرکت می‌کنند و افراد را آلوده می‌کنند و بنابراین باعث وبا می‌شوند. [^7] دکتر [ویلیام فار](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Farr "ویلیام فار") ، کمیسر سرشماری لندن در سال 1851 و عضو دفتر ثبت عمومی، معتقد بود که میاسما از خاک اطراف رودخانه تیمز ناشی می‌شود. این خاک حاوی مواد آلی در حال تجزیه است که حاوی ذرات میاسما هستند و در هوای لندن منتشر می‌شوند. نظریه‌پردازان میاسما به "پاکسازی و پاکسازی، به جای رویکرد علمی خالص‌تر میکروبیولوژی" اعتقاد داشتند. [^7] فار بعداً پس از انتشار آثار اسنو، با نظریه میکروبی اسنو موافقت کرد. [^8]

### نظریه میکروبی

در مقابل، نظریه میکروبی معتقد بود که علت اصلی وبا یک سلول میکروبی است که هنوز شناسایی نشده است. اسنو این نظریه را مطرح کرد که این میکروب ناشناخته از طریق نوشیدن آب از فردی به فرد دیگر منتقل می‌شود. [جان سایمون](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Simon_\(pathologist\) "جان سایمون (آسیب‌شناس)") ، آسیب‌شناس و مسئول ارشد پزشکی لندن، نظریه میکروبی اسنو را "عجیب و غریب" خواند. [^7]

گزیده‌ای از سخنان جان سایمون:

> این دکترین می‌گوید که وبا توسط یک «ماده‌ی بیماری‌زا» که از یک بیمار در حین دفع مدفوع خارج می‌شود و به طور تصادفی توسط افراد دیگر به عنوان آلودگی غذا یا آب بلعیده می‌شود، منتشر می‌شود؛ اینکه افزایش میکروب بلعیده شده‌ی بیماری در داخل معده و روده‌ها رخ می‌دهد و باعث ایجاد اعمال اساسی وبا، در ابتدا به عنوان یک اختلال موضعی، می‌شود؛ و اینکه «ماده‌ی بیماری‌زای وبا که خاصیت تولید مثل نوع خود را دارد، لزوماً باید نوعی ساختار، به احتمال زیاد یک سلول، داشته باشد.» [^7]

اگرچه سایمون نظریه اسنو را درک کرد، اما ارتباط آن با علت وبا را زیر سوال برد.

## شیوع بیماری در خیابان براد

در 31 آگوست 1854، پس از چندین شیوع دیگر در جاهای دیگر شهر، شیوع گسترده وبا در سوهو رخ داد. اسنو بعداً آن را «وحشتناک‌ترین شیوع وبا که تا به حال در این پادشاهی رخ داده است» نامید. [^9]

طی سه روز بعد، 127 نفر در خیابان براد یا نزدیک آن جان باختند. طی هفته بعد، سه چهارم ساکنان از منطقه فرار کردند. تا 10 سپتامبر، بیش از 300 نفر جان خود را از دست دادند و میزان مرگ و میر در برخی از نقاط شهر 12.8 در هر هزار نفر بود. [^10] تا پایان شیوع، 420 نفر جان خود را از دست دادند. [^11]

بسیاری از قربانیان به [بیمارستان میدلسکس](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex_Hospital "بیمارستان میدلسکس") منتقل شدند ، جایی که [فلورانس نایتینگل](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale "فلورانس نایتینگل") ، که برای مدت کوتاهی در اوایل سپتامبر به بیمارستان پیوست تا به شیوع بیماری کمک کند، بر روند درمان آنها نظارت داشت. طبق نامه‌ای از [الیزابت گاسکل](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Gaskell "الیزابت گاسکل") ، نایتینگل «از بعدازظهر جمعه (1 سپتامبر) تا بعدازظهر یکشنبه، شب و روز بیدار بود و از موجودات بیچاره (عمدتاً زنان افتاده آن محله - آنها بدترین شرایط را داشتند) که دائماً به آنجا آورده می‌شدند، پذیرایی می‌کرد - - لباس‌هایشان را درمی‌آورد - روی آنها [سنگ‌های تزئینی](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turpentine "تربانتین") می‌گذاشت و غیره، و خودش این کار را با هر کسی که می‌توانست انجام می‌داد.» [^12]

اسنو با صحبت با ساکنان محلی (با کمک [کشیش هنری وایتهد](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Whitehead_\(clergyman\) "هنری وایتهد (کشیش)") )، منبع شیوع بیماری را پمپ آب عمومی در خیابان براد (که اکنون [خیابان برادویک نام دارد](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadwick_Street "خیابان برادویک") ) در خیابان کمبریج شناسایی کرد. اگرچه بررسی شیمیایی و میکروسکوپی اسنو از نمونه‌ای از آب این [پمپ آب خیابان براد،](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho#Broad_Street_pump "سوهو") خطر آن را به طور قطعی اثبات نکرد، اما الگوهای بیماری و مرگ در میان ساکنان سوهو، مقامات کلیسای [سنت جیمز](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_St_James "وست‌مینستر سنت جیمز") را متقاعد کرد که پمپ را با برداشتن دسته آن از کار بیندازند. [^13]

اگرچه این اقدام به طور عمومی به عنوان پایان شیوع بیماری گزارش شده است، اما همانطور که اسنو توضیح می‌دهد، ممکن است این بیماری همه‌گیر از قبل به سرعت در حال کاهش بوده باشد:

> شکی نیست که همانطور که قبلاً گفتم، مرگ و میر به دلیل فرار جمعیت که اندکی پس از شیوع بیماری آغاز شد، بسیار کاهش یافت؛ اما حملات قبل از توقف استفاده از آب آنقدر کاهش یافته بود که نمی‌توان تصمیم گرفت که آیا چاه هنوز حاوی سم وبا در حالت فعال بوده یا به دلیلی آب عاری از آن شده است. [^14]

اسنو بعداً از یک [نقشه نقطه‌ای](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_map "نقشه نقطه‌ای") برای نشان دادن چگونگی وقوع موارد وبا در اطراف این پمپ استفاده کرد. [^15] تلاش‌های او برای ارتباط دادن شیوع وبا با منابع جغرافیایی بالقوه بر اساس چیزی بود که اکنون به عنوان [نمودار ورونوی](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voronoi_diagram "نمودار ورونوی") شناخته می‌شود. او پمپ‌های آب منفرد را نقشه‌برداری کرد و سلول‌هایی ایجاد کرد که نشان‌دهنده تمام نقاط روی نقشه‌اش بودند که به هر پمپ نزدیک‌تر بودند. بخشی از نقشه اسنو که نشان‌دهنده مناطقی در شهر است که نزدیکترین منبع آب موجود، پمپ خیابان براد بود، بیشترین میزان شیوع وبا را داشت. اسنو همچنین مقایسه آماری بین [شرکت آب‌رسانی ساوت‌وارک و واکسهال](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwark_and_Vauxhall_Waterworks_Company "شرکت آب‌رسانی ساوت‌وارک و واکسهال") و یک شرکت آب‌رسانی در [سیتینگ ولز](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seething_Wells "چاه‌های سیتینگ") (متعلق به [شرکت آب‌رسانی لمبت](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambeth_Waterworks_Company "شرکت آب‌رسانی لمبت") ) که در بالادست رودخانه قرار داشت و از این رو آب تمیزتری داشت، انجام داد. او نشان داد که خانه‌هایی که توسط اولی تأمین می‌شدند، 14 برابر بیشتر از خانه‌هایی که توسط دومی تأمین می‌شدند، میزان مرگ و میر ناشی از وبا داشتند. [^16] [^17]

اسنو در مورد کاهش موارد مربوط به پمپ خیابان براد گفت:

> مشاهده خواهد شد که در هر نقطه‌ای که ارسال آب به پمپ دیگری نسبت به پمپ خیابان براد به مراتب نزدیکتر می‌شود، مرگ و میر یا بسیار کاهش می‌یابد یا به کلی متوقف می‌شود. همچنین می‌توان متوجه شد که مرگ و میر در نزدیکی پمپ، جایی که دسترسی به آب آسان‌تر است، بیشتر است. [^18]

There was one significant anomaly: none of the workers in the nearby Broad Street brewery contracted cholera. As they were given a daily allowance of beer, they did not consume water from the nearby well.[^19] During the brewing process, the [wort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wort "مخمر") (un-fermented beer) is boiled in part so that [hops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hops "رازک") can be added. This step killed the cholera bacteria in the water they used to brew with, making it safe to drink. Snow showed that the Southwark and Vauxhall Waterworks Company was taking water from sewage-polluted sections of the Thames and delivering it to homes, resulting in an increased incidence of cholera among its customers. Snow's study is part of the history of [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health "بهداشت عمومی") and [health geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_geography "جغرافیای سلامت"). It is regarded as the founding event of [epidemiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology "اپیدمیولوژی").[^20]

In Snow's own words:

> On proceeding to the spot, I found that nearly all the deaths had taken place within a short distance of the \[Broad Street\] pump. There were only ten deaths in houses situated decidedly nearer to another street-pump. In five of these cases the families of the deceased persons informed me that they always sent to the pump in Broad Street, as they preferred the water to that of the pumps which were nearer. In three other cases, the deceased were children who went to school near the pump in Broad Street...
> 
> With regard to the deaths occurring in the locality belonging to the pump, there were 61 instances in which I was informed that the deceased persons used to drink the pump-water from Broad Street, either constantly or occasionally...
> 
> The result of the inquiry then was, that there had been no particular outbreak or prevalence of cholera in this part of London except among the persons who were in the habit of drinking the water of the above-mentioned pump-well.
> 
> I had an interview with the Board of Guardians of St. James's parish, on the evening of Thursday, the 7th September, and represented the above circumstances to them. In consequence of what I said, the handle of the pump was removed on the following day.[^21]

It was discovered later that this public well had been dug 3 feet (0.9 m) from an old [cesspit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesspit "چاه فاضلاب") that had begun to leak faecal bacteria. Waste water from washing [nappies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaper "پوشک") used by a baby who had contracted cholera from another source drained into this cesspit. Its opening was under a nearby house that had been rebuilt further away after a fire and a street widening. At the time there were cesspits under most homes. Most families tried to have their raw sewage collected and dumped in the Thames to prevent their cesspit from filling faster than the sewage could decompose into the soil.[^22]

At the same time, an investigation of cholera transmission was being conducted in [Deptford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deptford "دپتفورد"). Around 90 people died within a few days in that town, where the water was known to be clean, and there had been no previous outbreaks of cholera. Snow was informed that the water had recently turned impure. Before using it, residents had to let it run until the sudsy, sewer-like water turned clear. Snow, finding that the water the residents were using was no different from the usual water from their pump, determined that the outbreak must be caused by a leak in the pipes that allowed surrounding sewage and its contaminants to seep in to the water supply. This was similar to the Broad Street outbreak. The incoming water was being contaminated by the increasing levels of sewage, coupled with lack of safe plumbing.[^18]

After the cholera epidemic subsided, government officials replaced the Broad Street pump handle. They had responded only to the urgent threat posed to the population, and afterwards they rejected Snow's theory. To accept his proposal would have meant indirectly accepting the oral-faecal method of transmission of disease, which was too unpleasant for most of the public to contemplate.[^23]

## Investigation by John Snow

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Snow-cholera-map-1.jpg)

Original map by John Snow showing the clusters of cholera cases (indicated by stacked rectangles) in the London epidemic of 1854. The contaminated pump is located at the crossroads of Broad Street and Cambridge Street (now Lexington Street ), running into Little Windmill Street.

The Broad Street outbreak was an effect rather than a cause of the epidemic. Snow's conclusions were not predominantly based on the Broad Street outbreak, as he noted that he hesitated to come to a conclusion based on a population that had predominantly fled the neighbourhood and redistributed itself. He feared throwing off results of the study.[^18]

From a mathematical perspective, Snow's innovation was focusing on death rates in areas served by two water companies that drew water from the Thames, rather than basing it on data from victims of the Broad Street pump (which drew water from a well). Snow's work also led to a far greater health and safety impact than the removal of the Broad Street pump handle. Deactivating the pump "hardly made a dent in the citywide cholera epidemic, which went on to claim nearly 3,000 lives".[^24]

Snow was sceptical of the prevailing [miasma theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miasma_theory "نظریه میاسما"), which held that diseases such as cholera or the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "مرگ سیاه") were caused by pollution or a noxious form of "bad air". The [germ theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_theory_of_disease "نظریه میکروبی بیماری") was not yet established ([Louis Pasteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pasteur "لویی پاستور") proposed it in 1861). Snow did not understand the mechanism by which disease was transmitted, but the evidence led him to believe that it was not foul air. Based on the pattern of illness among residents, he hypothesized that cholera was spread by an agent in contaminated water.[^8] He first published his theory in 1849, in an essay titled "On the Mode of Communication of Cholera".[^25] In 1855 he published a second edition, including a more elaborate investigation of the effect of the water supply in the 1854 Soho outbreak.[^18]

The cholera epidemic of 1849–1854 was also related to the water supplied by companies in London at the time. The main players were the [Southwark and Vauxhall Waterworks Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwark_and_Vauxhall_Waterworks_Company "شرکت آب‌رسانی ساوت‌وارک و واکسهال") and the [Lambeth Waterworks Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambeth_Waterworks_Company "شرکت آب‌رسانی لمبت"). Both provided water to their customers drawn from the Thames, which was highly contaminated with visible and invisible products and bacteria. Dr Arthur Hill Hassall examined the filtered water and found it contained animal hair, among other foul substances. He remarked:

> It will be observed, that the water of the companies of the Surrey Side of London, viz., the Southwark, Vauxhall, and Lambeth, is by far the worst of all those who take their supply from the Thames [^18]

Other companies, such as the [New River Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_River_Company "شرکت نیو ریور") and [Chelsea Waterworks Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea_Waterworks_Company "شرکت آب‌رسانی چلسی"), were observed to have better filtered water; few deaths occurred in the neighbourhoods they supplied. They not only obtained their water from cleaner sources than the Thames, but filtered the water and treated it until there were no obvious contaminants.[^18]

As mentioned above, Snow is known for his influence on public health, which arose after his studies of the cholera epidemic. In attempting to figure out who was receiving impure water in each neighbourhood, what is now known as a double-blind experiment fell right into his lap. He describes the conditions of the situation in his essays:

> In many cases a single house has a supply different from that on either side. Each company supplies both rich and poor, both large houses and small; there is no difference in the condition or occupation of the persons receiving the water of the different companies...As there is no difference whatever either in the houses or the people receiving the supply of the two Water Companies, or in any of the physical conditions with which they are surrounded, it is obvious that no experiment could have been devised which would more thoroughly test the effect of water supply on the progress of Cholera than this, which circumstances placed ready made before the observer. The experiment too, was on the grandest scale. No fewer than three hundred thousand people of both sexes, of every age and occupation, and of every rank and station, from gentlefolks down to the very poor, were divided into two groups without their choice, and, in most cases, without their knowledge; one group being supplied water containing the sewage of London, and amongst it, whatever might have come from the cholera patients, the other group having water quite free from such impurity.[^18]

Snow went on to study the water contents from each home through a test performed on each sample. In this way, it could be deduced from which supplier the home received its water. He concluded that it was indeed impure water from the big companies that allowed cholera to spread rapidly. He proved this by observing London prisons, finding that cholera ceased there only a few days after switching to cleaner water sources.[^18]

## Snow's post-outbreak evaluation

Snow's analysis of cholera and cholera outbreaks extended past the closure of the Broad Street pump. He concluded that cholera was transmitted through and affected the alimentary canal within the human body. Cholera did not affect either the circulatory or the nervous system and there was no "poison in the blood...in the consecutive fever...the blood became poisoned from [urea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urea "اوره") getting into the circulation".[^26] According to Snow, this "urea" entered the blood through kidney failure. (Acute kidney failure is a complication of cholera.) [^27]

Therefore, the fever was caused by kidney failure, not by a poison already present in the subject's bloodstream. Popular medical practices, such as bloodletting, could not be effective in such a case. Snow also argued that cholera was not a product of Miasma. "There was nothing in the air to account for the spread of cholera".[^26] According to Snow, cholera was spread by persons ingesting a substance, not through atmospheric transmittal.[^26]

## Involvement of Henry Whitehead

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Whitehead_henry1884.jpg)

Rev. Henry Whitehead

The [Reverend Henry Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverend_Henry_Whitehead "کشیش هنری وایتهد") was an assistant [curate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curate "کیوریت") at St. Luke's church in Soho during the 1854 cholera outbreak.[^28]

A former believer in the [miasma theory of disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miasma_theory_of_disease "نظریه میاسما در مورد بیماری"), Whitehead worked to disprove false theories. He was influenced by Snow's theory that cholera spreads by consumption of water contaminated by human waste. Snow's work, particularly his maps of the Soho area cholera victims, convinced Whitehead that the Broad Street pump was the source of the local infections. Whitehead joined Snow in tracking the contamination to a faulty cesspool and the outbreak's [index case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_case "مورد شاخص") (the baby with cholera).[^29]

Whitehead's work with Snow combined [demographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics "جمعیت‌شناسی") study with scientific observation, setting an important precedent for epidemiology.[^30]

## Board of Health

The London Board of Health, which had recently been given new powers and was presided over by [Sir Benjamin Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Hall,_1st_Baron_Llanover "بنجامین هال، اولین بارون لانوور"), a Cabinet Minister, had several committees, of which the Committee for Scientific Inquiries was placed in charge of investigating the cholera outbreak. They were to study the atmospheric environment in London and to examine samples of water from several London water companies. The committee found that the most contaminated water supply came from the South London water companies, Southwark and Vauxhall.[^3] [^31]

As part of the Committee for Scientific Inquiries, Richard Dundas Thomson and Arthur Hill Hassall examined what Thomson called "vibriones". Thomson examined the occurrence of vibriones in air samples from various cholera wards and Hassall observed vibriones in water samples. Neither identified vibriones as the cause of cholera.[^3]

As part of its investigation of the cholera epidemic, the Board of Health sent physicians to examine in detail the conditions of the Golden Square neighbourhood and its inhabitants. The Board of Health ultimately attributed the 1854 epidemic to miasma.[^3]

## Dr Edwin Lankester's evaluation

Dr [Edwin Lankester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Lankester "ادوین لنکستر") was a physician on the local research conglomerate that studied the 1854 Broad Street cholera epidemic. In 1866, Lankester wrote about Snow's conclusion that the pump caused the outbreak. He agreed with Snow at the time, but his opinion, like Snow's, was not publicly supported. Lankester subsequently closed the pump due to Snow's theory and data on the pattern of infection, and infection rates dropped significantly. He was eventually named the first medical officer of health for [St James's parish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_St_James "وست‌مینستر سنت جیمز") in London, the area where the pump was.[^26]

## Broadwick Street pump in the 21st century

A replica pump was installed in 1992 at the site of the 1854 pump. Every year the John Snow Society holds "Pumphandle Lectures" on public health subjects. Until 2015, when the pump was removed due to redevelopment, it also held a ceremony there in which it removed and reattached the pump handle to commemorate Snow's discovery. The pump's original location is marked by a red granite paver, and plaques at the John Snow pub at the corner describe Snow's findings.[^32]
